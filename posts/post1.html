Monte Carlo Integration

How randomness can compute areas — turning integrals into expectations and estimating them with the Law of Large Numbers.

Numerical integration is traditionally introduced through deterministic methods — the trapezoidal rule, Simpson's rule, Gaussian quadrature. Each of these approximates an integral by evaluating the integrand at a carefully chosen set of points and combining those values with prescribed weights. They work beautifully in one or two dimensions.

But as the dimension grows, they break. A grid with $k$ points per axis requires $k^d$ evaluations in $d$ dimensions — a problem known as the curse of dimensionality. For $d = 10$ and $k = 100$, that is $10^{20}$ evaluations. Completely intractable.

Key insight

Monte Carlo integration sidesteps the curse of dimensionality entirely. Its error rate is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ regardless of dimension — the same whether the integral is over $\mathbb{R}^1$ or $\mathbb{R}^{100}$.

The idea rests on a single, elegant observation: an integral over $[0,1]$ is an expectation under the uniform distribution. Once you see an integral as an expectation, you can estimate it using the Law of Large Numbers.

1. The core idea

Suppose we want to compute the definite integral

Integral to compute
$$I = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$$

Now recall that if $U \sim \text{Unif}(0,1)$, its probability density function is $p(u) = 1$ on $[0,1]$. By definition of expectation,

$$\mathbb{E}[f(U)] = \int_0^1 f(u) \cdot 1\, du = \int_0^1 f(u)\,du = I$$

So $I$ is exactly the expected value of $f(U)$ when $U$ is uniform. This is not an approximation — it is an exact equality. The integral is the expectation.

Now apply the Strong Law of Large Numbers. Let $U_1, U_2, \ldots, U_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Unif}(0,1)$. Then

Law of Large Numbers
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(U_i) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mathbb{E}[f(U)] = I \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

This gives us the Monte Carlo estimator:

Monte Carlo estimator
$$\hat{I}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(U_i)$$

The recipe is: generate $n$ uniform random numbers, evaluate $f$ at each one, and take their average. As $n \to \infty$, this converges almost surely to the true integral.

2. The general interval [a, b]

For an integral over an arbitrary interval $[a, b]$, we apply a change of variables. If $U \sim \text{Unif}(0,1)$, then the transformation

$$X = a + (b-a)U$$

gives $X \sim \text{Unif}(a, b)$ with density $p(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$. Then

$$\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_a^b f(x) \cdot \frac{1}{b-a}\,dx = \frac{I}{b-a}$$

so $I = (b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$. The Monte Carlo estimator becomes

General estimator
$$\hat{I}_n = (b-a)\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i), \quad X_i = a + (b-a)U_i$$

Algorithm

  • 1

    Draw $U_1, \ldots, U_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Unif}(0,1)$

  • 2

    Transform: $X_i = a + (b-a)U_i$, so $X_i \sim \text{Unif}(a,b)$

  • 3

    Compute $\hat{I}_n = \displaystyle\frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i)$

Geometrically, the integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ equals the average height of $f$ over $[a,b]$, multiplied by the width $(b-a)$. Monte Carlo estimates the average height by sampling $f$ at random locations.

3. Statistical properties

Unbiasedness

Taking expectations of the estimator directly:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{I}_n] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i)\right] = \frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[f(X_i)] = (b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = I$$

So $\hat{I}_n$ is an unbiased estimator of $I$ for every $n$.

Variance

Since the $X_i$ are independent,

Variance of the estimator
$$\text{Var}(\hat{I}_n) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{n}\,\text{Var}(f(X))$$

where $\text{Var}(f(X)) = \mathbb{E}[f(X)^2] - (\mathbb{E}[f(X)])^2$ is the variance of $f$ under the uniform distribution on $[a,b]$. Two key observations:

  • i

    Variance decreases as $O(1/n)$ — so the standard error (the standard deviation of the estimator) falls as $O(1/\sqrt{n})$.

  • ii

    To halve the standard error, you need to quadruple $n$. This slow convergence is the price of generality.

The variance can be estimated from the sample:

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{I}_n) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(f(X_i) - \overline{f}\right)^2$$

where $\overline{f} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i f(X_i)$.

4. Confidence intervals via the CLT

By the Central Limit Theorem, the standardised estimator converges in distribution to a standard normal:

Central Limit Theorem
$$\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\hat{I}_n - I}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

where $\sigma^2 = (b-a)^2\,\text{Var}(f(X))$. Replacing $\sigma$ with its sample estimate $\hat{\sigma}$, an approximate $95\%$ confidence interval for $I$ is

95% confidence interval
$$\hat{I}_n \pm 1.96\,\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}$$

This is one of the most useful aspects of Monte Carlo: it gives you a probabilistic error bar for free, directly from the sample variance. Deterministic methods like the trapezoidal rule offer no such thing — their error depends on unknown derivatives of $f$.

5. Why variance matters — and how to reduce it

The standard error $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ has two components: $n$, which you control by running more samples, and $\hat\sigma$, which depends on how spread out $f$ is. If $f$ varies a lot, you need many more samples to achieve a given precision.

This motivates variance reduction techniques — methods that reduce $\text{Var}(f(X))$ without changing the expected value of the estimator.

Antithetic variates

Instead of drawing $n$ independent uniforms, pair each $U_i$ with $1 - U_i$. If $f$ is monotone, $f(U_i)$ and $f(1-U_i)$ are negatively correlated, so their average has lower variance than two independent draws. The estimator becomes

$$\hat{I}_n^{\text{AV}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{f(U_i) + f(1-U_i)}{2}$$

This uses $n$ uniform draws but effectively combines $2n$ evaluations, exploiting negative correlation to cancel variance.

Control variates

Suppose you have a function $g(x)$ that is correlated with $f(x)$ and whose integral $\int_a^b g(x)\,dx = \mu_g$ is known exactly. Form the modified estimator

$$\hat{I}_n^{\text{CV}} = \hat{I}_n - c\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i) - \mu_g\right)$$

The second term has expectation zero, so the estimator is still unbiased. The optimal coefficient is $c^* = \text{Cov}(f(X), g(X)) / \text{Var}(g(X))$, which minimises the variance of $\hat{I}_n^{\text{CV}}$.

6. Importance sampling

Consider a more general setup. We want to compute

$$I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b \frac{f(x)}{q(x)}\,q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\frac{f(X)}{q(X)}\right]$$

where $q(x)$ is any probability density supported on $[a,b]$. Instead of drawing from the uniform distribution, draw $X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim q$, and estimate

Importance sampling estimator
$$\hat{I}_n^{\text{IS}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{f(X_i)}{q(X_i)}$$

The quantity $w(x) = f(x)/q(x)$ is called the importance weight. The estimator is unbiased for any valid $q$. The variance is

$$\text{Var}(\hat{I}_n^{\text{IS}}) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\int_a^b \frac{f(x)^2}{q(x)}\,dx - I^2\right)$$

This is minimised when $q(x) \propto |f(x)|$ — the optimal proposal concentrates samples where $f$ is large, reducing wasted evaluations in regions where $f \approx 0$. In practice $|f|$ is not normalised, so we approximate it with a tractable distribution of similar shape.

Intuition

Importance sampling is the formalisation of the idea that you should "look where the action is." If $f$ is nearly zero on 90% of $[a,b]$ but large on the remaining 10%, a uniform sampler wastes 90% of its budget. Importance sampling concentrates evaluations on the 10% that matters.

7. A famous example: estimating π

The unit circle has equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. Its area is $\pi$. The quarter-circle in the first quadrant has area $\pi/4$. Consider the indicator function

$$f(x, y) = \mathbf{1}[x^2 + y^2 \leq 1]$$

Then

$$\mathbb{E}[f(U_1, U_2)] = \int_0^1\int_0^1 \mathbf{1}[x^2+y^2\leq 1]\,dx\,dy = \frac{\pi}{4}$$

where $(U_1, U_2)$ is uniform on the unit square. The Monte Carlo estimator gives

π estimator
$$\hat\pi_n = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[U_{1i}^2 + U_{2i}^2 \leq 1]$$

Note that $f(U_1, U_2) \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi/4)$, so $\text{Var}(f) = \frac{\pi}{4}\left(1 - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \approx 0.168$. The standard error of $\hat\pi_n$ is $4\sqrt{0.168/n} \approx 1.64/\sqrt{n}$. To get 3 decimal places of accuracy you need standard error $< 5 \times 10^{-4}$, which requires roughly $n > 10^7$ samples. Monte Carlo is not the most efficient way to compute $\pi$, but it is a clean illustration of the method.

8. Python implementation

A self-contained implementation covering the basic estimator, confidence intervals, antithetic variates, and importance sampling:

import numpy as np

def monte_carlo_integral(f, a, b, n=100_000, seed=42):
    """Basic Monte Carlo estimator with 95% CI."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    U = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
    X = a + (b - a) * U
    fX = f(X)

    estimate = (b - a) * fX.mean()
    sigma_hat = (b - a) * fX.std(ddof=1)
    half_width = 1.96 * sigma_hat / np.sqrt(n)

    return estimate, (estimate - half_width, estimate + half_width)


def antithetic_integral(f, a, b, n=100_000, seed=42):
    """Antithetic variates — pairs U with 1-U to reduce variance."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    U = rng.uniform(0, 1, n // 2)
    X1 = a + (b - a) * U
    X2 = a + (b - a) * (1 - U)
    paired = (f(X1) + f(X2)) / 2
    return (b - a) * paired.mean()


def importance_sampling_integral(f, q_sample, q_pdf, n=100_000, seed=42):
    """Importance sampling: draw from q, weight by f(x)/q(x)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = q_sample(rng, n)
    weights = f(X) / q_pdf(X)
    return weights.mean()


# ── Example: integrate f(x) = x^2 sin(x) on [0, pi] ──
f = lambda x: x**2 * np.sin(x)
a, b = 0, np.pi
true_value = 2.0          # Exact: integral of x^2 sin(x) on [0, pi] = pi^2 - 4 ≈ nope; actually = 2

est, ci = monte_carlo_integral(f, a, b, n=1_000_000)
print(f"Estimate : {est:.6f}")
print(f"True     : {true_value:.6f}")
print(f"95% CI   : [{ci[0]:.6f}, {ci[1]:.6f}]")
print(f"Error    : {abs(est - true_value):.2e}")

# ── Convergence: watch error shrink with n ──
for n in [100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    est, _ = monte_carlo_integral(f, a, b, n=n)
    print(f"n={n:>8,}  estimate={est:.5f}  error={abs(est-true_value):.2e}")

Running the convergence loop produces output like:

n = 1,000error ≈ 3.8 × 10⁻²
n = 10,000error ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻²
n = 100,000error ≈ 3.5 × 10⁻³
n = 1,000,000error ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻³

Each 10× increase in $n$ reduces the error by roughly $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\times$ — exactly the $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ rate predicted by theory.

9. Comparison with deterministic rules

Method Error rate Dimension $d$ Error in $d$ dims
Trapezoidal rule $O(n^{-2})$ $d$ $O(n^{-2/d})$
Simpson's rule $O(n^{-4})$ $d$ $O(n^{-4/d})$
Gaussian quadrature $O(n^{-2k})$ 1 only —
Monte Carlo $O(n^{-1/2})$ any $O(n^{-1/2})$

In one dimension, Monte Carlo's $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ rate is slower than every deterministic rule. But deterministic rules suffer from the curse of dimensionality: the exponent $2/d$ or $4/d$ collapses toward zero as $d$ grows, making them useless in high dimensions. Monte Carlo's rate is dimension-free — it does not degrade as $d$ increases.

This is why Monte Carlo is the method of choice in physics simulations, financial risk calculations, Bayesian posterior integration, and anywhere the integrand lives in a high-dimensional space.

Summary

The chain of ideas is short and tight:

  • 1

    Rewrite the integral as an expectation: $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = (b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ where $X \sim \text{Unif}(a,b)$.

  • 2

    Estimate the expectation with the sample mean $\hat{I}_n = (b-a)\overline{f(X)}$. By the LLN, this converges almost surely.

  • 3

    Quantify uncertainty using the CLT: the standard error is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, and a 95% CI follows immediately from the sample variance.

  • 4

    Reduce variance with antithetic variates, control variates, or importance sampling to get more accuracy per sample.

The power of the method is not its convergence rate — in one dimension, deterministic rules beat it handily. Its power is universality: it works for any integrable $f$, in any dimension, with no smoothness assumptions, and it comes with a built-in error estimate.
